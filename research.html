Job market paper

How Do Households Respond to Perceived Income Uncertainty?

Abstract

This paper studies how households’ consumption, saving, and debt repayment respond to perceived income uncertainty alone, separate from changes in expected income. A vignette experiment holds expected income constant and varies only perceived income uncertainty. Both the extensive and intensive margins of these decisions respond significantly and substantially. Precautionary saving and faster debt repayment operate as separate channels. In a within-subject design, choices respond to the current level of uncertainty rather than the path to that level. Liquidity and debt leverage are the main drivers of heterogeneous responses. The magnitude of uncertainty matters more than the ambiguity of its realization.

Household beliefs & portfolios

Intra-household Perceived Unemployment Risk and Portfolio Decisions

With Olga Goldfayn-Frank, Nathanael Vellekoop, and Michael Haliassos

Abstract

When a spouse can increase earnings during unemployment, theory predicts less precautionary saving and greater risk-taking. Households expecting partner support hold less liquidity, consistent with the precautionary-saving channel. However, stronger expected insurance is associated with lower risky asset holdings. Partners also exhibit an asymmetry in beliefs: individuals expect to provide more support than they expect to receive. Differences in labor market participation may leave insured households too financially constrained to take on portfolio risk. We are linking survey and administrative records to assess whether these beliefs are well-founded or systematically biased.

Professional expectations

more than moments: over-confidence and over-reaction of professional forecasters.

With Nils Eitner and Christian Schlag

Abstract

Standard tests of forecaster rationality, such as the Coibion–Gorodnichenko (CG) regression, use only the mean forecast. Two forecasters with the same mean revision therefore look identical, even if they revise their uncertainty very differently. Smooth diagnostic expectations (Bianchi et al., 2024) predict overreaction in both the mean and the uncertainty. Mean-based tests cannot detect the second distortion. We propose a new methodology: a distributional CG regression. It tests how each forecaster revises the mean and the uncertainty jointly, using reported probabilities directly. We measure revisions and errors with the Wasserstein distance and split it into signed shift and dispersion components (Resin et al., 2024). In simulations, the mean-based CG regression cannot separate overconfident forecasters from rational ones. The decomposed regression separates over/(under)reaction and over/(under)confidence from rationality. For validating our method empirically, we apply the method to HICP inflation forecasts from the ECB Survey of Professional Forecasters (1999–2025). Results show with the method, ECB forecasters are both overreactive and overconfident, more so for upward than for downward revisions.
